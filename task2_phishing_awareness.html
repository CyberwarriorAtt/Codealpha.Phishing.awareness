Don't take the bait.

A short, hands-on module on recognising phishing emails and fake websites, the tricks attackers use on people, and what to do about it. About 10 minutes.

1. What is phishing?

Phishing is a message that pretends to be someone you trust (a bank, a boss, a delivery company) to make you click a link, open a file, or hand over a password. It works because it targets people, not software.

Social engineering is the psychology behind it. Attackers rely on:

  • Urgency: "Your account will be closed in 24 hours."
  • Fear: "Suspicious login detected."
  • Authority: a fake email from the CEO or IT department.
  • Greed or curiosity: prizes, refunds, "see who viewed your profile".
  • Helpfulness: "Can you quickly buy gift cards for me?"

    4. Fake websites: what to look for

    • Misspelled or lookalike domains (arnazon.com, paypa1.com) and extra words like -secure or -login.
    • A padlock only means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is honest. Phishing sites use HTTPS too.
    • Login pages you reached through an email link instead of typing the address yourself.
    • Poor design, low-quality logos, spelling mistakes, and pop-ups asking for card or ID details.

    5. Best practices

    • Pause. Urgency is the attacker's main weapon.
    • Verify separately. Open the official app or type the site yourself, or call a known number.
    • Hover before you click (long-press on mobile) to see the real link.
    • Turn on multi-factor authentication so a stolen password isn't enough.
    • Use a password manager; it won't autofill on a fake domain.
    • Never open unexpected attachments, especially .exe, .zip, .html, or macro-enabled Office files.
    • Report it to your IT/security team or email provider, then delete it.
    • If you clicked: change the password, enable MFA, tell IT, and watch your accounts.

    6. Real-world examples

    Google/Facebook invoice fraud (2013 to 2015). A scammer sent fake invoices from a lookalike vendor and collected over $100 million from both companies before being caught.
    Twitter hack (2020). Attackers phoned employees pretending to be IT support, got credentials, and took over high-profile accounts. No malware was needed.
    Everyday smishing. "Your parcel is held, pay a small fee" text messages that lead to a fake courier page collecting card details.
